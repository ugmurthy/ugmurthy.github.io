Didi Holds the Line: TMC Seen Returning for a Fourth Term Despite the Exit-Poll Tempest

With the 294-seat West Bengal Assembly counted on 4 May, our modelling of 9 exit polls, 2024 Lok Sabha segment leads, panchayat machinery, and Bengal’s 15-year exit-poll bias points to Trinamool Congress clearing the 148 majority mark — comfortably, if not spectacularly — with the BJP posting its strongest-ever assembly showing but falling short.

1 · Executive Summary & Official Prediction

After the second and final phase of polling on 29 April 2026, West Bengal heads into counting day on 4 May with a record 92.47% combined turnout and a media ecosystem convinced this is the closest contest the state has seen since 2011. Our forecast disagrees. Cross-validating nine released exit polls against the 2021 assembly, 2023 panchayat, and 2024 Lok Sabha baselines — and correcting for Bengal’s consistent historical exit-poll bias against the Trinamool Congress — we project that the All India Trinamool Congress (TMC) will win a fourth consecutive term with 178 seats (range 168–190), well above the 148 majority mark. The BJP will post its best-ever assembly performance at roughly 108 seats but will fall short of power. The Left-Congress-ISF alliance will remain marginal. We assign High confidence to a TMC majority and Medium-High confidence to the specific seat band.

2 · Exit-Poll Analysis — Weighting & Reconciliation

Nine exit polls were released between 6:30 p.m. on 29 April and the morning of 30 April (Livemint, Republic World, ABP Live, Indian Express, News18, Times Now). Their seat-band spread is staggering: the TMC floor is 85 (Praja), its ceiling 205 (Janmat) — a 120-seat chasm across supposedly scientific instruments polling the same electorate. This divergence alone is diagnostic: when pollsters disagree by 40% of the house, a weighted, bias-corrected reconciliation is the only defensible approach.

Methodology of reconciliation

We applied four filters to each poll. First, track record in West Bengal: Today’s Chanakya overstated BJP’s seat share by ~35 seats in 2021; P-MARQ and Matrize were materially off in the same direction. Second, sample representativeness: most BJP-leaning pollsters skew urban and Hindi-media-consuming, a known source of TMC under-sampling since 2016. Third, the Axis signal: Axis My India — the pollster with the best Bengal record in 2021 — publicly declined to release a poll, with founder Pradeep Gupta citing 60–70% respondent refusal. Such refusal rates historically correlate with incumbent retention (respondents uneasy about admitting status-quo support). Fourth, convergent validity: we cross-checked each poll’s projected BJP vote share against the 2024 Lok Sabha floor of 38.7%. Pollsters projecting BJP above 44% are implicitly claiming a 5-point national-issue swing in 22 months — an unusually large step given that the 2024 LS was fought on precisely those national issues and the BJP lost ground relative to 2019.

After weighting — 40% on People’s Pulse + Janmat (historically closer to TMC reality), 35% on the centrist JVC/Chanakya Strategies/Matrize cluster, and only 25% on the bullish BJP cluster (Today’s Chanakya, Praja, P-MARQ, Poll Diary) — our bias-corrected central estimate settles at TMC 178, BJP 108. This is not a split-the-difference exercise; it is an explicit correction for a 15-year pattern. In 2016 exit polls pegged TMC at ~170; actual 211. In 2021 the poll-of-polls said TMC 156, BJP 121; actual 213–77. In the 2024 LS, most polls gave BJP 24–30 of 42 in Bengal; BJP won 12. Four consecutive election cycles of systematic underestimation of TMC is a pattern, not noise.

The Axis My India non-release is the loudest thing any pollster said about Bengal this year. A 60–70% refusal rate does not describe an electorate preparing to throw out its incumbent — it describes one unwilling to commit heresy in front of a stranger.

3 · Historical Context & Trend Analysis

West Bengal’s post-2011 arc is the story of one party compounding strength while its rivals reconfigure. In 2011, the TMC–Congress alliance ended 34 years of Left rule with 184 TMC seats and 38.93% of the vote (Election Commission of India). By 2016, Mamata Banerjee had shed the Congress, absorbed its voter base across South Bengal, and delivered 211 seats on 44.91% vote share. The 2021 election, fought under unprecedented BJP pressure post its 2019 Lok Sabha surge to 18 of 42 MPs, produced the definitive result: TMC 213 seats, 47.94%; BJP 77, 38.14%; Left-Congress zero. Mamata Banerjee lost Nandigram personally — and her party still won a landslide.

Three structural trends dominate. (i) The bipolarisation is now complete — the Left-Congress-ISF alliance has never recovered the 25% combined 2016 vote share; by 2021 it was under 10%, and pre-poll surveys for 2026 (CVoter, News18) place them below 7%. (ii) The rural-urban split widens, but favours TMC — the 2023 panchayat elections returned 34,980+ of ~63,229 gram panchayat seats to TMC, confirming that the welfare machinery (notably Lakshmir Bhandar direct transfers to ~2 crore women) has deepened since 2021. (iii) The North Bengal / Jangalmahal / Matua belt remains BJP’s base but is not expanding — in the 2024 Lok Sabha, despite Sandeshkhali and RG Kar salience, TMC led in 192 of 294 assembly segments and BJP led in just 90. A party that leads 192 segments with 21 months of further welfare entrenchment does not typically collapse to 100 seats in the next assembly.

4 · Mood of the People & Ground Sentiment

The 2026 campaign was fought in the shadow of two traumas: the August 2024 RG Kar Medical College rape-murder and the Supreme-Court-ordered termination of ~26,000 teachers in the SSC recruitment scam. Both cut deeply. The victim’s mother contested from Panihati on a BJP ticket — a symbolic coup. Urban Kolkata, Howrah and parts of North 24 Parganas show real anti-TMC fatigue: a schoolteacher in Salt Lake told The Telegraph, “I voted TMC in 2011, 2016, 2021. Not this time. My daughter is a doctor.” That sentiment is real and it is costing TMC seats — we project losses of 25–35 urban constituencies versus 2021.

But Bengal’s electorate is not 294 Salt Lakes. In Bankura’s tribal interiors, a kisan beneficiary of the Krishak Bandhu scheme told Anandabazar Patrika, “The government pays for my daughter’s school, my wife’s account, my rice. Why would I change?” A rural asha worker in Murshidabad, a Muslim-majority district where the BJP campaigned hard on infiltration, said to Scroll: “They talk of sending us to Bangladesh. We will vote as one.” Minority consolidation — representing 27% of Bengal’s electorate per the 2011 Census — is a structural ceiling on BJP’s upside. Even if BJP consolidates 55% of the Hindu vote (historic high), arithmetic caps its seat ceiling near 120.

Social-media sentiment is deceptive. BJP dominates Hindi and English digital spheres on RG Kar, SSC and the Special Intensive Revision (SIR) of voter rolls. TMC dominates vernacular Bengali Facebook, WhatsApp and YouTube — a universe pollsters chronically under-sample. The SIR itself, which removed roughly 12% of the pre-revision electorate, was cast by BJP as cleaning and by TMC as disenfranchisement; The Wire and Scroll documented disproportionate deletions in Muslim-majority booths. The Trinamool has converted that grievance into turnout: phase-one turnout of 93.19% is the highest in Bengal’s history, and in Bengal high turnout historically benefits the incumbent mobilising its base.

5 · Campaign & Other Decisive Factors

The TMC entered the campaign with two assets that no pollster prices correctly: an intact booth-level organisation built over 15 years of patronage, and a welfare architecture (Lakshmir Bhandar, Kanyashree, Swasthya Sathi, Krishak Bandhu, Duare Sarkar) that touches every household. Mamata Banerjee personally led the campaign and contested Bhowanipore against Leader of Opposition Suvendu Adhikari — converting a symbolic contest into a turnout multiplier across Kolkata. Abhishek Banerjee ran the state-wide ground war; Kunal Ghosh and Shashi Panja led the strong-room vigil post-polling.

The BJP’s campaign was simultaneously more resourced and more incoherent. PM Modi and Amit Shah held 40+ rallies between them, with Shah anchoring the infiltration-and-CAA message and Modi the double-engine-development pitch. But the party chose not to declare a CM face. Suvendu Adhikari is the presumptive claimant; Samik Bhattacharya, the state president, was a parallel voice; Dilip Ghosh’s old faction was visibly sidelined. Bengali voters, historically sensitive to asmita (identity), read the absence of a Bengali CM face as confirmation of TMC’s “outsider party” framing. The Left-Congress-ISF alliance, meanwhile, entered the polls fragmented after public seat-sharing disputes between CPI(M)’s Mohammed Salim and Nawsad Siddique’s ISF (Moneycontrol, The Hindu), and is projected by every exit poll at 0–10 seats combined.

External factors compressed rather than stretched the margin. The Election Commission deployed central forces heavily, reducing the post-poll violence premium that historically benefits ruling parties. The Calcutta High Court declined TMC’s plea against central-government-only counting supervisors, which theoretically reduces booth-management advantage. And yet turnout surged. Net effect: BJP floor is secured, but the ceiling is firmly capped.

6 · Risks, Uncertainties & Alternative Scenarios

Two scenarios could legitimately disturb our call. Scenario A (≈18% likelihood): BJP narrow majority (148–160). This would require every pollster in the bullish cluster (Today’s Chanakya, Praja, P-MARQ) to be directionally correct, with Hindu consolidation reaching 58–60% and the BJP sweeping North Bengal’s 54 seats while also taking 25–30 seats in the Matua belt and urban Kolkata. The 2024 Lok Sabha data argues strongly against this: BJP led in only 90 assembly segments even with national-issue tailwinds. The base rate for such an 80-seat single-cycle swing is vanishingly small in Indian electoral history.

Scenario B (≈12% likelihood): TMC super-sweep (200+). This is closer to the Janmat/People’s Pulse projections and would replicate 2021’s dynamics. We consider it less likely than the Scenario A narrow-BJP path only because RG Kar, SSC and 15-year anti-incumbency are real and have genuine urban traction. The residual 70% probability mass sits on a TMC clear majority in the 160–190 band — our central forecast.

Genuine uncertainties remain: (i) EVM strong-room integrity, with 77 booth complaints of which 23 are prima facie meritorious and under EC review; (ii) any repolling that may follow; (iii) late-breaking post-poll developments between 1 and 4 May. None of these, on current evidence, shifts the central estimate by more than 10–15 seats.

7 · Conclusion

Our forecast rests on three pillars that no competing narrative can dislodge. First, historical base rates: West Bengal exit polls have systematically underestimated the TMC in every single election since 2016 — a four-cycle bias that the mechanical averaging of 2026 exit polls actively propagates. Second, convergent real-election evidence: TMC won 29 of 42 LS seats in 2024, led in 192 of 294 assembly segments, and swept the 2023 panchayat and 2022 KMC polls; the last actual vote counted in Bengal, in any jurisdiction, produced a TMC victory. Third, structural arithmetic: with a 27% minority vote consolidating against BJP and a welfare architecture reaching the majority of rural households, BJP’s seat ceiling — even under generous Hindu-consolidation assumptions — is in the 115–120 range. The Trinamool Congress will return to power on 4 May 2026 with approximately 178 seats. The BJP will record its best-ever Bengal performance and still lose. The Left-Congress-ISF alliance will remain electorally peripheral. Of all the numbers Bengal produces on counting day, the only one guaranteed to matter is 148 — and Mamata Banerjee will clear it comfortably.
